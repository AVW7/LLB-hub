## Q1 — Client wants a prescribed debt collected (4)

> [!question] Question
> The Prescription Act provides that most monetary claims are "extinguished" three years after the creditor became aware of the claim. A client approaches you to collect a debt which has clearly prescribed. What advice do you give regarding: 1.1 your ethical duty (2); 1.2 prospects of success (1); 1.3 cost implications (1)?

> [!note]- Self-check pointers (not an official memo)
> Advise candidly that the claim has prescribed before doing further work — you may not conceal this to earn a fee. Prospects of success are poor, since prescription is a complete defence once pleaded. Advise on the likely costs of pursuing a claim likely to fail on a special plea of prescription, including possible adverse costs. See [[Prescribed Claims — Ethical Duty and Negligence Liability]].

## Q2 — Attorney negligently allows a claim to prescribe (7)

> [!question] Question
> A client's file is misplaced and you negligently allow the claim to prescribe. What is your position relating to: 2.1 liability for the client's loss (1); 2.2 advice to the client (2); 2.3 further prosecution of the claim (2); 2.4 any protection for you (2)?

> [!note]- Self-check pointers (not an official memo)
> You/your firm are liable to the client for the loss. Advise the client to consult another attorney to pursue a claim against you. The underlying claim is dead; what proceeds is the negligence claim against you. Do not admit liability — refer the claim to the Attorneys' Indemnity Insurance Fund (AIIF), which could repudiate if liability were admitted. See [[Prescribed Claims — Ethical Duty and Negligence Liability]].

## Q3 — Trust funds held idle for over a year (5)

> [!question] Question
> You must hold a substantial amount in trust for a client which will not be needed for more than a year. How do you act professionally in relation to: 3.1 the funds being idle for a long period, and how you give effect to your advice to the client (3); 3.2 an offer by the client to make you a short-term loan at favourable rates (2)?

> [!note]- Self-check pointers (not an official memo)
> 3.1 Advise the client and arrange for the funds to be placed in a separate interest-bearing account for the client's own benefit, rather than left in the general trust account. 3.2 Raises a conflict of interest — not advisable; if you proceed, the client must obtain independent legal advice on the transaction. See [[Trust Account Rules — Deposits and Fee Transfers]], [[Borrowing From or Lending to Clients]].

## Q4 — Deposit for costs in advance (5)

> [!question] Question
> You request a client to make a deposit for costs in advance. Once made: 4.1 does the deposit become the practitioner's property? (1) 4.2 which bank account must it be deposited into? (1) 4.3 when can it be used as fees? (1) 4.4 can you transfer an estimated fee amount from trust to business account mid-matter? (1) 4.5 if you may not, what are the consequences of doing so anyway? (1)

> [!note]- Self-check pointers (not an official memo)
> The identical question is answered in full at [[PLE 2016-06-28 Special Supp]] A4: no; the trust account; only once fees are due and your books written up (unless interim fees agreed); no; doing so anyway is unprofessional conduct and exposes you to a charge of theft of trust monies. See [[Trust Account Rules — Deposits and Fee Transfers]].

## Q5 — Grounds to refuse a mandate (2)

> [!question] Question
> Name two circumstances when an attorney is obliged to refuse a mandate.

> [!note]- Self-check pointers (not an official memo)
> Any two of: conflict of interest; illegality/fraud requested by the client; lack of competence/expertise; animosity; unavailability. See [[Mandate Acceptance, Refusal and Withdrawal]].

## Q6 — Duty re adverse SC judgments (2)

> [!question] Question
> During a civil trial you become aware of recent SC judgments adverse to your client's case. What obligation do you have towards the court in this regard?

> [!note]- Self-check pointers (not an official memo)
> You are bound to bring adverse authority to the court's attention; no similar obligation exists towards your colleague other than in the course of argument. See [[Duty of Candour to the Court]].
