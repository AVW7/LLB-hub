## Q1 — Adverse authority and magistrate recusal (8)

> [!question] Question
> 1.1 During a civil trial an attorney becomes aware of recent SCA judgments adverse to his/her client's argument. What obligation does the attorney have towards the court and towards his/her opponent? (3)
> 1.2 Just before a criminal trial before magistrate X, it appears the complainant is the magistrate's son. Discuss the appropriate steps to ensure the ethical rules are applied correctly. (5)

> [!success]- Answer
> 1.1 An attorney is bound to bring any adverse authority to the notice of the court, but need not inform the colleague other than in the course of argument.
> 1.2 The magistrate should be requested to recuse himself. Before proceedings start, approach the magistrate in chambers to point out the apparent conflict of interest. Should the magistrate not accede in chambers, make a formal application in court. Should that also fail, proceed with the case and take the matter on review or appeal if the client is convicted.

Related: [[Duty of Candour to the Court]], [[Recusal of a Judicial Officer]]

## Q2 — Commissioner of oaths, firm interest (5)

> [!question] Question
> You have completed an affidavit for an urgent High Court application, ready for attestation. The only commissioner of oaths available is a senior partner at your firm.
> 2.1 May your partner act as commissioner of oaths? Motivate. (3)
> 2.2 Are there exceptions to this rule? List them. (2)

> [!success]- Answer
> 2.1 No. Clients are deemed represented by the whole firm. No one with an interest in the matter may act as commissioner of oaths — the partner is deemed to have an interest, so may not act, however urgent.
> 2.2 Conveyancing transactions; formal affidavits for government records.

Related: [[Commissioner of Oaths Restrictions]]

## Q3 — Refusal grounds and confidentiality exceptions (5)

> [!question] Question
> 3.1 List four situations when an attorney is obliged to refuse a mandate. (2)
> 3.2 List three instances when attorney/client confidentiality does not apply. (3)

> [!success]- Answer
> 3.1 Conflict of interest; client wants you to do something illegal/improper; you lack the expertise; you have too much work.
> 3.2 Any 3: legislation (FICA); client informs you he/she is going to commit a crime; the client's name; knowledge of an asset in a deceased client's estate the executor is unaware of; information obtained with your own senses.

Related: [[Mandate Acceptance, Refusal and Withdrawal]], [[Client Confidentiality and Privilege]]

## Q4 — Competing mandates (4)

> [!question] Question
> A client approaches you with instructions. Before you accept, another client approaches with instructions producing a larger profit. Due to time constraints you cannot accept both. Must you accept the first mandate, or are you free to choose either? Motivate.

> [!success]- Answer
> The general rule is that a practitioner is entitled to accept or refuse any work offered unless for special reason(s) there is some obligation to accept or refuse it. The practitioner is free to accept whichever mandate he/she chooses. However, once a mandate is accepted it must be completed — though there are circumstances when an attorney may timeously withdraw.

Related: [[Mandate Acceptance, Refusal and Withdrawal]]

## Q5 — Borrowing money from a client (3)

> [!question] Question
> May an attorney borrow money from an existing client? Discuss.

> [!success]- Answer
> Not proper, unless the client is in the business of money lending. If not in that business and you decide to continue, the client must obtain independent legal advice on the money lending transaction — potential conflict of interest.
