## Q1 — "Fit and proper" definition (2)

> [!question] Question
> When is a practitioner considered a "fit and proper" person?

> [!success]- Answer
> A practitioner is a fit and proper person if his/her personal qualities reflect integrity and reliability.

Related: [[Admission as an Attorney and Fitness to Practise]]

## Q2 — Accepting a mandate — is it ever obligatory? (4)

> [!question] Question
> 2.1 Is a practitioner obliged to accept a client's mandate all the time? (1)
> 2.2 Name 3 aspects a practitioner must consider before accepting a client's mandate. (3)

> [!success]- Answer
> 2.1 No.
> 2.2 Any 3: possibility of a conflict of interest arising; animosity between attorney and client; competence to handle the type of case; availability; whether the client wants the attorney to break the law or commit fraud.

Related: [[Mandate Acceptance, Refusal and Withdrawal]]

## Q3 — FICA identification steps (4)

> [!question] Question
> In terms of FICA all practitioners are accountable institutions. Name the prescribed steps you must take before establishing a business relationship or concluding a transaction with a client.

> [!success]- Answer
> (a) Establish and verify the client's identity. (b) If the client acts for another, establish and verify that other person's identity and the client's authority to act for them. (c) If another person acts for the client, establish and verify that other person's identity and their authority to act for the client.

Related: [[FICA Obligations on Attorneys]]

## Q4 — Contingency fee agreement (5)

> [!question] Question
> A Plaintiff client wants to sue a Defendant for R1 million on a money lending transaction, and wants your fees dealt with on the basis that you are entitled to remuneration only if the claim is successful.
> 4.1 Are you permitted to enter into such an agreement? (1)
> 4.2 If yes, what are the prescribed formalities? (2)
> 4.3 What maximum fee would you be entitled to charge? (2)

> [!success]- Answer
> 4.1 Yes, an attorney is permitted to enter into a contingency fee agreement.
> 4.2 The agreement must be in writing and in the form prescribed by the Contingency Fees Act.
> 4.3 Up to twice the usual fee, not exceeding 25% of the amount awarded.
>
> Marker's note preserved in the archive: the position isn't uniform — the Law Society of the Northern Provinces accepted members may choose between a Contingency Fees Act agreement or a common-law contingency arrangement. Full credit was given for all correct answers.

Related: [[Contingency Fee Agreements]]

## Q5 — Striking off for DUI (3)

> [!question] Question
> You are convicted of driving under the influence of liquor. The Law Society applies to have your name struck from the roll. What is the High Court likely to do, and why?

> [!success]- Answer
> The High Court is not likely to strike an attorney from the roll for an offence such as DUI, as the test is whether the individual remains a fit and proper person to practise. The court will probably still award costs to the Society, as it performs a fiduciary duty. If the offence involved dishonesty, this would clearly affect fitness to practise and the attorney would probably be struck or suspended.

Related: [[Admission as an Attorney and Fitness to Practise]]

## Q6 — Commissioner of oaths, own firm (2)

> [!question] Question
> A partner in your firm requests you: 6.1 to administer the oath in a conveyancing matter — may you? (1) 6.2 to administer the oath if it is not a conveyancing matter — may you? (1)

> [!success]- Answer
> 6.1 No. 6.2 Yes.

> [!note] Discrepancy flagged
> These answers appear reversed relative to the general no-in-firm-interest rule and the conveyancing exception stated in [[PLE 2015-11-14]] A2 (which says conveyancing is where an in-firm commissioner **is** permitted). See [[Commissioner of Oaths Restrictions]] for how this note reconciles the two papers — treat [[PLE 2015-11-14]] as the controlling formulation of the rule.

Related: [[Commissioner of Oaths Restrictions]]

## Q7 — Secretary theft of trust funds (5)

> [!question] Question
> You discover theft of R100 000 by your secretary from your firm's trust account, in a particular file.
> 7.1 Can your client institute a claim against you or your firm for the stolen money, and can you/your firm be held liable? (2)
> 7.2 Would the position differ if a candidate attorney or professional assistant had stolen the funds? Yes/no. (1)
> 7.3 With whom can your client lodge a claim if you/your firm are financially unable to repay? (1)
> 7.4 Within how many months must he lodge that claim? (1)

> [!success]- Answer
> 7.1 Yes — the client can institute a civil claim against the attorney or firm, and they can be held liable.
> 7.2 No.
> 7.3 Attorneys Fidelity Fund.
> 7.4 6 months.
