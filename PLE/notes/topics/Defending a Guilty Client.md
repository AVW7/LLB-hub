# Defending a Guilty Client

The flagship PLE ethical dilemma — a client admits guilt to you in consultation but instructs you to plead not guilty. Tested with a different charge each sitting (armed robbery, rape, reckless/negligent driving) but the answer structure is identical.

## Core position

- **Yes, you may represent the client and enter a plea of not guilty.** (Confirmed directly in [[PLE 2016-05-28]] Q1.1 and [[PLE 2015-05-30]] Q1.1 — both answered "Yes.")
- Accepting the mandate does **not** mean you may present a false case. The ethical limits attach to *how* you run the defence, not to *whether* you may run it.

## Ethical limits on how you conduct the defence

From [[PLE 2015-05-30]] Q1.2:
- You may **not** lead evidence, and may **not** put allegations to witnesses, that conflict with the version the client actually gave you.
- You **may** test the credibility and weight of the State's evidence — e.g. cross-examine on disputed issues like identification of the accused — without putting a false version.

From [[PLE 2016-05-28]] Q1.2 (rape charge), the same principle applied step-by-step through a trial:
- Enter the not-guilty plea (exercise the right to silence as to the client's own admission — s115 CPA).
- Cross-examine State witnesses to test their credibility, but never put false statements to them.
- If the defence does not succeed in a **s174 discharge** at the close of the State's case, close your own case **without leading any evidence**, and argue only on the weaknesses of the State's case as led.

## Related principle: advising on a lawful "loophole"

[[PLE 2016-06-15 Supp]] Q7 asks whether it is improper for a practitioner to advise a client on how to take advantage of a loophole in the law — answered **No**. This sits alongside the guilty-client rule: zealous, technically-correct advocacy within the law is proper; manufacturing a false factual version is not.
