# Commissioner of Oaths Restrictions

Who may (and may not) administer an oath for your own firm's client — a small but recurring rule, always tested with the same "may a partner in your firm act as commissioner?" structure.

## The general rule

No one with an interest in the matter may act as commissioner of oaths. A partner (or anyone else) in the firm instructed on the matter is **deemed to have an interest** — because the client is deemed to be represented by the whole firm, not just the individual attorney handling the file — and so **may not** commission an affidavit for that client, no matter how urgent. See [[PLE 2015-11-14]] A2.1.

## Exceptions

Two recognised exceptions where a partner/colleague in the same firm **may** commission the oath:
- **Conveyancing transactions**
- **Formal affidavits for government records**

Source: [[PLE 2015-11-14]] A2.2.

This is confirmed the other way round at [[PLE 2015-11-26 Supp]] Q6/A6: asked "may you administer the oath in a **conveyancing** matter for your firm?" → **No** is given as the answer to 6.1 in that paper's numbering quirk — read together with [[PLE 2015-11-14]], conveyancing is the exception that *permits* an in-firm commissioner; treat [[PLE 2015-11-14]] A2.2 as the controlling statement of the exception and use [[PLE 2015-11-26 Supp]] only to confirm the general no-interest rule for non-conveyancing matters (**Yes** was given there for a non-conveyancing matter at A6.2, which is inconsistent with the general rule — flag this discrepancy and default to the [[PLE 2015-11-14]] formulation: firm-interest bars a partner from commissioning **except** for conveyancing/government-record affidavits).
