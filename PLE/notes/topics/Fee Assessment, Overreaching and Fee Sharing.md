# Fee Assessment, Overreaching and Fee Sharing

Covers three related but distinct fee-ethics questions: what makes a fee "fair and reasonable" (assessor's checklist), what "overreaching" means, and the rules on splitting fees with candidate attorneys, counsel, or non-lawyers who refer work.

## Factors an assessor considers ("fair and reasonable" fee)

Tested near-verbatim in three sittings — any 5 of:

1. The amount and importance of the work done
2. The complexity of the matter, or the difficulty/novelty of the work or questions raised
3. The skill, labour, specialised knowledge and responsibility involved
4. The number and importance of documents prepared or perused (not just their length)
5. The place and circumstances in which the services were rendered
6. The time expended
7. Where money/property is involved, its amount or value
8. The importance of the matter to the client
9. The quality of the work done
10. The experience or seniority of the practitioner

Sources: [[PLE 2015-06-11 Supp]] A5, [[PLE 2016-06-28 Special Supp]] A1, [[PLE 2019-02-03 Assignment]] Q5.1 (no memo — self-check against the identical list above).

## Overreaching

[[PLE 2019-02-03 Assignment]] Q5.2 (no memo — self-check): overreaching is charging a client a fee that is excessive/unconscionable relative to the work actually done — i.e. the opposite of the "fair and reasonable" standard above. It is misconduct and exposes the practitioner to disciplinary sanction and a reduction of the fee on taxation/assessment.

## Fee sharing

[[PLE 2016-05-28]] Q4.4:
- **May you share fees with your candidate attorney?** No.
- **May you pay your candidate attorney commission based on fees they generate?** Yes.
- **May you share fees with Counsel?** No.

## Referral fees / touting

[[PLE 2019-02-03 Assignment]] Q4.4.2 and Q4.4.3 (no memo — self-check): Counsel offers to give your firm "a percentage of his fees" out of gratitude for the referral, and separately your office manager wants payment for having referred a client to you. Both raise the same problem — attorneys may not pay or receive **referral fees/commissions for touting for work** (a related but distinct rule from the candidate-attorney commission above, which is a *remuneration* arrangement for work actually performed, not a referral kickback). Reason from the fee-sharing prohibition above and the profession's general prohibition on touting.
