# Admission as an Attorney and Fitness to Practise

Covers the requirements for admission, the "fit and proper person" standard, and how that standard is applied when a practitioner is later convicted of an offence and the Law Society seeks to have them struck from the roll.

## Requirements to satisfy the Court on admission

[[PLE 2015-06-11 Supp]] Q2/A2 — any 4 of:
- Fit and proper person
- SA citizen, or permanent resident
- SA LLB qualification, or a qualification from a designated country
- Served the required period of articles, or exempted therefrom
- Passed the attorneys admission examination

## What is a "fit and proper" person?

> "A practitioner is a fit and proper person if his/her personal qualities reflect integrity and reliability." — [[PLE 2015-11-26 Supp]] A1

## Fidelity Fund Certificate requirements

[[PLE 2015-06-11 Supp]] Q3/A3 — before issuing a Fidelity Fund Certificate (needed to practise for your own account), the Law Society will require (any 4, half mark each):
- Details of your trust bank account
- FICA registration
- Address and contact details
- Membership fees (paid up)
- Names of all professionals in the practice
- Details of appointed auditors

## Consequence of not paying Law Society membership fees

[[PLE 2016-05-28]] A4.2 — you will not be allowed to practise or to charge fees.

## Striking off — conviction not involving dishonesty (e.g. drunk driving)

Tested near-identically in three sittings:

> "The High Court is not likely to strike the name of an attorney from the roll for an offence such as driving under the influence of alcohol/liquor, as the test to be applied is whether the individual is a fit and proper person to practise. The court will probably nevertheless award costs to the Society, as it is performing a fiduciary duty. If an offence were to include an element of dishonesty, this would clearly affect the fitness of the person to practise, who would probably be struck or suspended." — [[PLE 2016-06-15 Supp]] A1, restated at [[PLE 2015-11-26 Supp]] A5

So the rule has two limbs: (1) a conviction alone doesn't automatically mean unfitness — the test is whether the person remains fit and proper; (2) **dishonesty is the dividing line** — an offence involving dishonesty is much more likely to lead to striking/suspension than one that doesn't (like a DUI).

[[PLE 2019-02-03 Assignment]] Q13 (no memo — self-check) asks the same DUI scenario **and** explicitly asks whether the position would differ if the offence involved dishonesty — apply the two-limb rule above.

## Striking off / refusal to endorse admission — ongoing intentional law-breaking

[[PLE 2016-06-15 Supp]] Q4/A4: candidate attorney convicted during articles for possession/use of cannabis, declares intention to **continue** breaking the law on religious grounds; Law Society refuses to endorse the admission application.

> "The Court would likely hold that you are not a fit and proper person to be admitted, the reason being that as long as the prohibition on the use or possession of cannabis remains on the statute books, you would constantly be breaking the law and that this would bring the profession into disrepute."

The distinguishing feature from the DUI scenario above is the **stated intention to keep offending** — ongoing, deliberate illegality (even without dishonesty) undermines fitness to practise because it signals disregard for the law itself.
