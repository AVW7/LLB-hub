# Mandate Acceptance, Refusal and Withdrawal

The single most heavily tested PLE topic — some version of "when must you refuse / withdraw from a mandate" appears in almost every sitting.

## The general rule

> "The general rule is that a practitioner is entitled to accept or refuse any work offered to him/her unless for special reason(s) there is some obligation to accept or refuse work. The practitioner is free to accept whichever mandate he/she chooses. However, once a mandate is accepted, it must be completed. There are however circumstances when an attorney may timeously withdraw." — [[PLE 2015-11-14]] A4, restated verbatim in [[PLE 2016-06-28 Special Supp]] A3

This is the answer used whenever a paper poses the "two competing mandates, which must you take" scenario ([[PLE 2015-11-14]] Q4, [[PLE 2016-06-28 Special Supp]] Q3): you are free to choose either, subject to the refusal grounds below.

## Grounds to refuse a mandate

Tested repeatedly, with a consistent (if not perfectly identical) list across papers:

- **Conflict of interest**, or the possibility of one arising
- **Illegality** — client wants you to break the law or commit fraud
- **Lack of competence/expertise** for the matter
- **Animosity** between attorney and client, or a foreseeable breakdown of trust
- **Unavailability** — you would not be able to give the matter proper attention (too much work / time constraints)

Sources: [[PLE 2016-05-28]] A2 ("Illegality, conflict of interest and competency"); [[PLE 2015-06-11 Supp]] A6; [[PLE 2016-06-15 Supp]] A5; [[PLE 2015-11-26 Supp]] A2.2 (any 3 of 5).

## Grounds to withdraw from an already-accepted mandate

[[PLE 2015-05-30]] Q3/A3 (asked as "obliged to *terminate*" rather than refuse) gives a related but distinct list — any three of:
1. Conflict of interest arises or becomes known
2. Animosity / breakdown of trust arises
3. Lack of expertise on issues that develop during the matter
4. Failure by the client to provide instructions
5. Failure by the client to pay an agreed deposit

## Related conflict-of-interest fact pattern in CCP

Note the criminal-procedure angle on the same underlying problem — see CCP's `Legal Representation of Multiple Accused` note, which covers an attorney who cannot represent two co-accused with conflicting statements and, once briefed, cannot simply drop one and keep the other. That is the CCP-side procedural consequence; this note (and [[Conflict of Interest]]) covers the PLE-side ethical rule that drives it.
