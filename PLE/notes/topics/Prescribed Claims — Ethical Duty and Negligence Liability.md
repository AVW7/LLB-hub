# Prescribed Claims — Ethical Duty and Negligence Liability

Two related but distinct fact patterns, both turning on the Prescription Act's three-year extinction of most monetary claims: (1) a client wants you to pursue a claim you can see is already prescribed, and (2) *you* negligently let a client's claim prescribe.

## Scenario 1 — client instructs you on a claim that has already prescribed

[[PLE 2015-12-02 Special Supp]] Q1 (no memo — self-check) and [[PLE 2019-02-03 Assignment]] Q1 (no memo — self-check) ask the same three/four-part structure:
- Your **ethical duty** — advise the client candidly that the claim has prescribed before doing any further work; you may not conceal this to earn a fee.
- **Prospects of success** — poor to none, since prescription is a complete defence if pleaded.
- **Cost implications** — advise the client of the likely costs of pursuing a claim that will probably fail on a special plea of prescription, and of an adverse costs order if it does.
- (2019 paper adds) **The presiding officer** — note that prescription must be specially pleaded by the defendant; a court will not raise it mero motu, so technically the claim could still proceed if the defendant does not plead it.

## Scenario 2 — you negligently allow a client's claim to prescribe

[[PLE 2015-12-02 Special Supp]] Q2 (no memo — self-check) and [[PLE 2019-02-03 Assignment]] Q2 (no memo — self-check), also cross-referenced from [[Trust Account Theft and the Attorneys Fidelity Fund]]'s "files went missing" fact pattern:
- **Liability for the client's loss** — yes, you/your firm are liable to the client for the loss caused by your negligence.
- **Advice to the client** — advise the client to consult **another attorney**, who will pursue a claim against you (the negligent attorney) on the client's behalf.
- **Further prosecution of the claim** — the underlying claim itself is dead (prescribed); what is "prosecuted" instead is the **negligence claim against you**.
- **Protection for you** — do **not** admit liability (this can void cover); the claim against you is referred to the **Attorneys' Indemnity Insurance Fund (AIIF)**, which could repudiate if you admitted liability.
- (2019 paper adds) **The Law Society and misconduct** — negligently prescribing a claim is potentially reportable/disciplinary; and **fees/lien on documents** — consider whether you can still claim fees for work done and whether you may exercise a lien over the client's documents pending payment, weighed against your negligence exposure.

## Related principle in the trust-theft scenario

The "files went missing, claims prescribed" thread inside [[Trust Account Theft and the Attorneys Fidelity Fund]] (e.g. [[PLE 2016-06-15 Supp]] A2.2) uses the **identical AIIF mechanism** described above — same remedy, different cause (theft-driven file loss rather than straightforward attorney negligence).
