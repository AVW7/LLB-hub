# Universal Partnership and Breach of Promise to Marry

## Core framework

- **Tacit universal partnership — *Butters v Mncora* (SCA):** a universal partnership of all property (*societas universorum bonorum*) between unmarried cohabitants need not be express — it can arise tacitly from the parties' conduct. The test is whether it is more probable than not that a tacit agreement was reached. The "partnership enterprise" need not be a purely commercial undertaking — it can extend to both the family life and a business, so a partner's *non-financial* contribution (running the home, raising children, foregoing her own career to support the other's business) can found the required contribution even where she earned little or no income herself. Long cohabitation, sustained domestic/childcare contribution, and sharing in the benefits of the other partner's income all support a claim; the counter-argument is that the business remained substantially one partner's individual achievement and that the normal incidents of cohabitation, without more, are equivocal evidence of a shared-profit intention.
- **Breach of promise to marry — *Van Jaarsveld v Bridges* (SCA):** two distinct claims are available. (a) An *actio iniuriarum* for sentimental damages, but **only** if the repudiation was contumelious (wrongful in the delictual sense, *animo iniuriandi*) — hurt feelings alone do not suffice. (b) A contractual claim, but limited to *actual* losses reasonably within the contemplation of the parties (e.g. wedding costs already incurred, a job or opportunity relinquished in reliance on the promise) — prospective losses (what the marriage itself would have brought, e.g. anticipated financial security) are generally too speculative to be recoverable.

## Recognising the fact pattern

A long-term unmarried cohabitation that ends (often via one partner's infidelity or a broken engagement) triggers both analyses together: first assess whether a tacit universal partnership existed (property claim), then separately assess whether the manner of ending the relationship was objectively wrongful/insulting enough to found an *actio iniuriarum*, and whether any *actual* (not speculative) reliance losses were incurred.
