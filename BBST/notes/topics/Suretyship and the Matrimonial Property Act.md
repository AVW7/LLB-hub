# Suretyship and the Matrimonial Property Act

## Core framework

- **s15(2)(h) of the Matrimonial Property Act 88 of 1984** requires a spouse married **in community of property** to obtain the other spouse's **written consent** before binding themselves as surety for a third party's debt.
- **s15(6) exemption:** the consent requirement does not apply to a suretyship entered in the ordinary course of the surety's profession, trade or business (*Amalgamated Banks of SA Bpk v De Goede*).
- **s15(9) — the creditor's position:** a creditor relying on a suretyship need only show the surety had full contractual capacity to sign — it is *not* part of the creditor's cause of action to prove the surety was unmarried, or that the other spouse's consent was given or was unnecessary. The burden of raising and proving a lack of consent (where relevant) falls on the surety/spouse resisting enforcement, not the creditor.
- **The consent requirement only arises where the marriage is in community of property.** Spouses married out of community of property (with or without accrual) act independently and s15 simply does not apply. Parties who are not married at all (e.g. unmarried life partners) likewise fall outside s15 entirely. A customary marriage's proprietary consequences are governed separately by s7 of the Recognition of Customary Marriages Act, so whether s15 applies to a customary spouse depends on that marriage's own property regime.

## Recognising the fact pattern

The trigger fact is a spouse (or life partner) signing as surety for a business debt **without telling** the other spouse/partner — the analysis is a short, mechanical walk through: (1) is there a marriage, and if so what regime? (2) does s15(2)(h) even apply? (3) does the s15(6) ordinary-course-of-business exemption apply? (4) what would the creditor actually need to prove under s15(9)?
