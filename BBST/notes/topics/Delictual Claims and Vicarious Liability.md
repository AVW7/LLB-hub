# Delictual Claims and Vicarious Liability

## Core framework

- **Negligence — the *Kruger v Coetzee* test** (1966 (2) SA 428 (A)): would a reasonable person in the defendant's position have foreseen the reasonable possibility of injury/loss, taken reasonable steps to guard against it, and did the defendant fail to take those steps?
- **Vicarious liability:** an employer may be held liable for an employee's delict committed in the course and scope of employment. Apply the connecting-factor analysis (drawing on the *Midway Two Engineering* line of authority): is there an employment-type relationship, and is there a sufficiently close link between the delict and the employer's business, instructions or interests? Negligent driving on the way to work, for example, is not automatically "in the course and scope" of employment merely because the employer supplied the vehicle.
- **Strict liability — *actio de pauperie*:** an animal's owner is strictly liable for injury the animal was **not reasonably expected to cause**, without needing to prove owner fault, once the plaintiff proves the animal acted contrary to its nature.
- **Subrogated claims:** where an insurer has indemnified its insured for damage caused by a third party's negligence, the insurer steps into the insured's shoes (subrogation), but the action is still brought and pleaded in the *insured's* name — there is no direct contractual link between the wrongdoer and the insurer, and the insured cannot recover the same loss twice.
- **Letter of demand:** not a legal or common-law prerequisite to a cause of action, but sending one can affect costs recovery (a prompt payment on receipt of summons may still earn the plaintiff costs) and affects when mora interest starts running — the summons itself can constitute a valid demand.
- **Contributory factors (e.g. intoxication):** a factor like a claimant's blood-alcohol level is only relevant if it is *causally* connected to the collision (e.g. if the claimant's vehicle was in motion and driving erratically) — where the claimant's vehicle was stationary and lawfully positioned, an elevated blood-alcohol reading does not, on its own, defeat or reduce the claim.
- **Professional negligence by the attorney:** where an attorney has missed a prescription deadline or otherwise mishandled a client's claim through their own fault, the attorney must not conceal the negligence from the client, must advise the client to seek independent legal advice or refer the matter elsewhere, and should point the client toward the Attorneys Fidelity Fund/professional indemnity cover rather than personally acknowledge negligence.

## Recognising the fact pattern

These questions typically pair an ordinary negligence/vicarious-liability analysis with either (a) a subrogated insurer's claim, (b) an *actio de pauperie* animal-injury variant, or (c) a professional-negligence twist where the candidate's own firm delayed instructions and part of the claim has prescribed.
