# Lease Agreements and Eviction

## Core framework

- **Recovering arrear rental + eviction from commercial premises** — a combined summons/particulars of claim typically pleads: the lease agreement (attached, or an explanation for why it is not attached — see the Rule 6(6) point in [[Pre-Trial Procedure and Expedited Judgment]]); the tenant's breach (non-payment); cancellation (or reliance on the summons itself as notice of cancellation); the arrears calculation; any additional damages flowing from the breach or holding-over (e.g. itemised repair costs for unauthorised alterations/damage, municipal arrears, a tracer's fee to locate an absconding tenant); and prayers for arrears, damages, ejectment, interest and costs.
- **Every item of damage should be separately itemised and quantified** — the archive consistently rewards pleading specific, itemised repair quotes and municipal arrears rather than a lump-sum damages figure.
- **Costs orders** on an eviction/arrears claim should be motivated on the facts (e.g. attorney-and-client costs where the lease itself provides for them, or where the tenant's conduct was egregious).
- **Evicting an unlawful occupier who is not a party to the original lease** (e.g. someone found occupying the property after the actual tenant absconds) falls under the **Prevention of Illegal Eviction from and Unlawful Occupation of Land Act (PIE)**, not ordinary contractual eviction procedure — PIE requires a Notice of Motion (supported by an affidavit) rather than simple ejectment relief tacked onto the arrears claim.
- **A holding-over tenant** (remaining in occupation after the lease term expires with no new agreement concluded) is generally still bound by the expired lease's terms on a month-to-month basis until validly cancelled or the tenant is lawfully evicted.

## Recognising the fact pattern

A recurring near-identical fact pattern appears across sittings: a landlord (often a CC) sues a commercial tenant for arrears and eviction, the tenant has caused property damage through unauthorised repair/alteration work, and — in the richer variants — an unrelated third party is later found occupying the premises after the original tenant vanishes, requiring a separate PIE application.
