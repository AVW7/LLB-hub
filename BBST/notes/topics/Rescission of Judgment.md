# Rescission of Judgment

## Core framework

- **Rule 42** — rescission of a judgment erroneously sought or erroneously granted in a party's absence, containing a patent error, or granted by common mistake between the parties.
- **Rule 31(2)(b)** — rescission of a default judgment, applied for within 20 days, requiring the applicant to show *good cause*: (a) a reasonable, non-wilful and non-reckless explanation for the default, and (b) a bona fide defence to the claim with reasonable prospects of success.
- **Common law** rescission is also available outside the strict Rule 42/31(2)(b) requirements in appropriate cases.
- **Sequestration/liquidation orders specifically** — s149(2) of the Insolvency Act empowers the court to rescind or vary its own sequestration order, but only where **unusual, special or exceptional circumstances** are shown (*Abdurahman v Estate Abdurahman* 1959 (1) SA 872 (C)); there is no fixed time limit for a s149(2) application, unlike Rule 31(2)(b)'s 20-day window.
- **Mala fides defeats rescission:** conduct suggesting the applicant tried to delay or defeat creditors (e.g. failing to disclose pending sequestration proceedings to a divorce court, or timing a divorce to shield assets) points away from "exceptional circumstances" and toward dismissal — the court may instead vary the order *mero motu* under Rule 42 (e.g. to sequestrate individual estates post-divorce) rather than rescind it outright.
- **Costs on a rescission application** may be ordered party-and-party, attorney-and-client, no order, or reserved, depending on the parties' conduct and the reasons for the default.
- On the merits side, rescission of a *default judgment for non-payment* commonly turns on whether the debtor has a genuine, provable defence (e.g. proof of payment, missing only a reference number) and a credible explanation for missing the deadline (e.g. correspondence between attorneys that was overlooked).

## Recognising the fact pattern

Two distinct fact patterns recur: (1) rescinding a **sequestration/liquidation order** obtained where the debtor later claims the timing (e.g. of an intervening divorce) undermines the order — apply s149(2) and *Abdurahman*, not Rule 31(2)(b); (2) rescinding an ordinary **default judgment for an unpaid debt** where correspondence between attorneys broke down — apply Rule 31(2)(b)'s two-part good-cause test.
