# Wills and Disqualified Beneficiaries

## Core framework

- **s4A(1) of the Wills Act 7 of 1953** disqualifies anyone who attests/signs a will as a witness — or who writes it out (amanuensis) — and the spouse of such a person, from receiving a benefit under that will.
- **s4A(2)(a)** allows a court to declare the disqualified person competent to receive the benefit anyway, if satisfied they did not defraud or unduly influence the testator when the will was executed. At common law, a person who wrote the will purely at the testator's dictation, gaining nothing extra by doing so, has historically been permitted to inherit (*In re Estate Maxwell*) — evidence of dictation without self-dealing supports competence under s4A(2)(a).
- **s2B — the divorce-deeming provision:** where a testator dies within **three months** of a divorce, any bequest to the by-then-former spouse is deemed to have been revoked, *unless* the will shows an intention to benefit the ex-spouse regardless of the divorce. If death occurs *after* the three-month window, s2B does not apply and the bequest stands as originally drafted.
- **s2(3) — condonation of a non-compliant document as a will:** a court may order the Master to accept a document that does not comply with the Act's formalities (e.g. unsigned) as a valid will, if satisfied the deceased drafted or executed it and intended it to be their will (*Letsekga v The Master*; *Ex parte Maurice*; *Thirion v Die Meester*). The greater the departure from the formalities, the stronger the evidence of genuine testamentary intent must be.
- **Marriage/divorce does not automatically revoke a will** — a will remains valid until formally revoked; only the s2B deeming provision above creates an automatic (rebuttable) exception, and only within its three-month window.
- **No presumption of simultaneous death** exists in South African law by reference to age or sex — where spouses die close in time, each death must be individually established and sequenced on the facts before determining who inherited from whom.

## Recognising the fact pattern

A "disqualified beneficiary" question usually layers two or three of these rules at once: a will witnessed/written by the intended beneficiary (s4A), a testator who died shortly after a divorce (s2B), and/or a later informal document (email, dictated note) competing with a formal earlier will (s2(3)). Work through each will/document separately before concluding who ultimately inherits, and check whether an application to rescind an earlier competence order affects the outcome (see [[Rescission of Judgment]]).
