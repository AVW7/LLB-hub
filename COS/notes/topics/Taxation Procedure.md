# Taxation Procedure

The mechanics of getting a bill of costs before the **Taxing Master** ([[Costs Terminology]]) and the rapid-fire true/false facts examiners build around it.

## Notice of Taxation

Before a bill can be taxed, the party wishing to tax it must give the opponent notice. A **Notice of Taxation** must state: the matter (case number, parties), that it is the [Plaintiff/Defendant]'s Party and Party Bill of Costs that will be taxed, the Taxing Master's room/venue, the date and time (or "so soon thereafter as the matter may be heard"), and that the opponent may attend and may be represented by an attorney. It is addressed to the Clerk of the Court and to the opponent's attorney.

- **Notice period**: there is **no fixed statutory period** (e.g. not "5 days") — the requirement in the High Court is simply **reasonable notice**.

## True/false facts worth memorising verbatim

- A Taxing Master in the High Court may allow the qualifying expenses of an expert on taxation **not only** where there is a court order to that effect, **but also** where there is an agreement between the parties to that effect.
- A litigant unhappy with a Taxing Master's decision **cannot appeal** it — the Rules only provide for a **Review** procedure.
- If **more than 20%** of the number of items or value on a Bill of Costs (excluding expenses) is taxed off, the Taxing Master **may disallow the fees for drawing the bill and for attending the taxation**.
- An attorney **may** accept a deposit against future work to be done and disbursements to be incurred — the amount must be **deposited in trust** until the work is done or the disbursement is paid.
- Where a court wishes to convey its displeasure at the conduct of the attorney of record, it can order costs **de bonis propriis** (see [[Costs Terminology]]).
- An attorney may **share fees with any practitioner**, including a correspondent.
- A **cost consultant who is not an attorney** may **not** appear before a taxing master in the High Court and tax a bill of costs on behalf of an attorney in the attorney's absence.
