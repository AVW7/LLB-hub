# Access to the Police Docket

## Leading case

**Shabalala and 5 Others v Attorney-General of the Transvaal and Another** — the right to a fair trial (originally s25(3) of the interim Constitution, now **s35(3)** of the Constitution) ordinarily includes access to witness statements and such docket contents as are relevant to properly exercising that right.

## In practice

The attorney writes to the prosecutor requesting copies of witness statements / a request for further particulars. The prosecutor obtains the docket from the investigating officer, makes copies, and advises the attorney of the copying cost.

## Grounds on which the State may resist access

1. Access is not justified for the purposes of a fair trial.
2. Reasonable risk that access would disclose the identity of an informer or state secrets.
3. Reasonable risk that access would lead to the intimidation of witnesses or otherwise prejudice the proper ends of justice.
4. An accused in a **bail application** is not entitled to copies of the docket.

Whether the State can justify denial depends on the circumstances of each case.

## Remedy if the State refuses

The attorney may formally **apply to court** to enforce disclosure (the Shabalala remedy). The trial court has a discretion to order access after balancing the danger of disclosure against the danger that a fair trial will otherwise be impossible. The State bears the onus of justifying its refusal on one of the grounds above.

## Caution

The defence must not misuse docket access to manufacture a defence built around the State's own evidence.
