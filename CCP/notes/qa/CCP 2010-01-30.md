## Q1 — Police bail vs prosecutor bail (2)

> [!question] Question
> In certain instances bail can be granted by a police officer or a public prosecutor before an accused person's first appearance in court.
> 1.1 "Police bail" can be granted for which offences?
> 1.2 A prosecutor can grant bail for which offences?
> (Do not list all the offences but only the schedules concerned.)

> [!success]- Answer
> 1.1 Section 59 CPA: a police officer may grant bail before first appearance for any offence **other than** those in Part II or Part III of Schedule 2, after consulting the investigating officer.
> 1.2 Section 59A CPA: a prosecutor may authorise release on bail for offences listed in **Schedule 7**.

> [!question] Question

## Q4 — Section 60(4) bail requirements (5)

> [!question] Question
> Your client was arrested for robbery and applies for bail. List the requirements set out in section 60(4) CPA.

> [!success]- Answer
> Bail must be refused where there is a likelihood the accused, if released, will:
> - endanger the safety of the public/a particular person, or commit a Schedule 1 offence;
> - attempt to evade trial;
> - attempt to influence or intimidate witnesses, or conceal/destroy evidence;
> - undermine or jeopardise the proper functioning of the criminal justice system, including the bail system; or
> - (in exceptional circumstances) disturb the public order or undermine public peace/security.

Related: [[Bail Applications]]

## Q5 — Seizure of articles under the CPA (4)

> [!question] Question
> The SAPS are entitled to seize certain articles in terms of the CPA. Discuss briefly with reference to the legislation.

> [!success]- Answer
> **Section 20 CPA** — the State may seize anything ("an article") which:
> (a) is concerned, or on reasonable grounds believed to be concerned, in the commission or suspected commission of an offence (in or outside the Republic);
> (b) may afford evidence of the commission or suspected commission of an offence; or
> (c) is intended to be used, or on reasonable grounds believed to be intended to be used, in the commission of an offence.

Related: [[Seizure of Articles]]

## Q6 — After-hours bail and the 48-hour rule (4)

> [!question] Question
> John Smith is arrested for fraud (R25,000) at 17:00 on Thursday 24 December. The investigating officer has no objection to bail and a prosecutor is willing to come out after hours.
> 6.1 Can John be released on bail by the prosecutor in these circumstances?
> 6.2 If the IO/prosecutor object after reading the docket, when MUST John appear in court?

> [!success]- Answer
> 6.1 No — fraud where the amount exceeds R20,000 is not an offence for which "police bail" (s59) or "prosecutor's bail" (s59A, Schedule 7) can be granted; it falls into a more serious category requiring a **Magistrate** to grant bail.
> 6.2 Section 50 CPA — the 48-hour period expires Saturday 17:00; because courts are closed over the weekend, John must be brought before court **before close of court the following Monday**.

Related: [[Bail Applications]], [[Securing Attendance of the Accused]]

## Q7 — Options after an unsuccessful bail application (2)

> [!question] Question

> [!success]- Answer

## Q10 — Appeal vs review, four differences (8)

> [!question] Question
> List four differences between an appeal and a review.

> [!success]- Answer
> Appeal: against a judgment/conviction; limited to the record; limited to grounds of appeal; specific time periods.
> Review: against procedure; evidence may be tendered by affidavit; any ground of review; within a reasonable time.

Related: [[Appeal vs Review]]

## Q11 — Grounds to object to a charge, s85 (5)

> [!question] Question
> Section 85 CPA deals with the accused's right to object to a charge before pleading under section 106. List the five grounds.

> [!success]- Answer
> (a) charge does not comply with the CPA's requirements for the essentials of a charge; (b) does not set out an essential element of the offence; (c) does not disclose an offence; (d) lacks sufficient particulars of a matter alleged (proviso: not available where accused must plead under s119/122A in the Magistrate's Court); (e) accused not correctly named or described.

Related: [[Objection to a Charge]]

## Q12 — Procedure for raising a s85 objection (5)

> [!question] Question
> What are the provisos/requirements regarding raising the objections in Q11? How does the attorney go about raising them?

> [!success]- Answer
> The accused must give **reasonable notice** to the prosecution of the intention to object and state the grounds. The prosecution (AG/Prosecutor) may waive the notice requirement; the court may, on good cause, dispense with notice or adjourn the trial to allow notice to be given.

Related: [[Objection to a Charge]]

## Q13 — Sections 77 and 78 enquiries (5)

> [!question] Question
> Sections 77 and 78 CPA deal with two kinds of enquiries held under section 79. Discuss both.

> [!success]- Answer
> **s77** — capacity to understand proceedings: if it appears the accused, by reason of mental illness/defect, is incapable of understanding the proceedings so as to make a proper defence, the court directs an enquiry/report under s79. Legal representative must raise this as early as possible.
> **s78** — criminal responsibility at the time of the offence: whether the accused, due to mental illness/defect at the time of the act, was incapable of appreciating its wrongfulness or of acting in accordance with that appreciation.

Related: [[Mental Capacity Enquiries]]

## Q14 — Bail test: Schedule 5 vs Schedule 6 (3)

> [!question] Question
> The test for bail differs for Schedule 5 and Schedule 6 offences. What is the test for each, and is it proved beyond reasonable doubt or on a balance of probabilities?

> [!success]- Answer
> Schedule 5: whether it is in the **interests of justice** to release the accused on bail. Schedule 6: whether there are **exceptional circumstances** permitting release. Both are decided on a **balance of probabilities** (as in civil matters).

Related: [[Bail Applications]]

> [!question] Question
> Mr A was arrested and charged with assault common. He admits assaulting the complainant but claims self-defence. Prepare a plea of not guilty incorporating formal admissions setting out the defence.

> [!success]- Answer
> Full **Section 115** statement: sound and sober senses, understanding of the charge, plea of not guilty to common assault, formal admission of the act of striking the complainant, narrative establishing unlawful attack by the complainant (produced a knife) and that the accused acted throughout in **self-defence** (private defence).
