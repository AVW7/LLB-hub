## Q1 — Withdrawal for non-payment of a fee deposit (4)

> [!question] Question
> You are consulted by Mr A who was involved in a motor collision with Mr B. He is being charged with reckless or negligent driving. If A fails to pay you the agreed deposit on fees, may you withdraw? Discuss.

> [!success]- Answer
> You may withdraw provided you have placed the client on terms (i.e. made clear that unless the deposit is paid by a certain date you will withdraw) and you do so at an appropriate time (at a stage where the client can still obtain other representation).

Related: [[Accepting, Refusing and Withdrawing from a Mandate]]

## Q2 — Murder client admits guilt, instructs not-guilty plea (5)

> [!question] Question
> You are approached by a client who has been charged with murder. The client instructs you that he is responsible for the death of the victim. However, on account of a previous conviction for murder, he wishes you to plead not guilty to the charge. As you are not averse to accepting the mandate, discuss briefly how you as an attorney would execute your mandate and how you are limited in your defence.

> [!success]- Answer
> An attorney is permitted to act in a matter where the client indicates that he is responsible for the death of the victim on a charge of murder. It is not for the attorney to determine the guilt or innocence of the client, as this would amount to a usurpation of the function of the court. However, in pleading not guilty, the attorney must ensure that he does not in any way mislead the court in any submissions he makes, and also does not mislead the witness or witnesses by, inter alia, making submissions which he knows are untrue, or by the postulation of an affirmative defence which is also not true. However, it is open to the attorney to attack the credibility or reliability of the witnesses and raise technical defences, like the jurisdiction of the court and the correctness of the charge or charges framed against the accused.

Related: [[Defending a Client Who Admits Guilt]]

## Q3 — Three ethical scenarios (11)

> [!question] Question
> Discuss briefly how you as an attorney would act in the following circumstances:
> 3.1 You realise in a civil matter that the other party's attorney has issued a subpoena for a witness and you would like to consult with that witness. (3)
> 3.2 You are instructed to act in a defamation action. Upon considering the facts of the case you advise the client that there is little or no prospect of success in the proposed exercise. Nevertheless your client is adamant that you proceed with the proposed action. As predicted the action fails. Upon receipt of your account the client disputes the amounts. (4)
> 3.3 You are instructed by a client who is charged with murder to represent him in the criminal trial. He admits that he has committed the deed in question but wishes to plead not guilty. You are inclined to accept the mandate. (4)

> [!success]- Answer
> 3.1 If the other party has issued a subpoena for a specific witness it denotes that the civil matter in question has been set down for trial. The rule regarding interviewing a witness of another practitioner after the action has commenced but before evidence is led at trial is that the practitioner seeking the interview must give notice to his opponent, by whatever means reasonable in the circumstances, of his intention to conduct such an interview. Consent is not required.
> 3.2 The practitioner is ethically obliged to advise the client of the costs implications. The client is obliged to pay the practitioner on either an attorney-and-client scale or attorney-and-own-client scale. In addition, because there is little or no prospect of success, there is a strong likelihood an order of costs may be granted against the client, possibly including costs on an attorney-and-client scale. Upon the client disputing the account, the practitioner is entitled to draw a Bill of Costs on the applicable scale of fees and have it taxed by either the Clerk of the Court in the Magistrate's Court or the Taxing Master in the High Court.
> 3.3 If the practitioner elects to represent the client on the basis of a not-guilty plea, he may do so provided that in the conduct of the case he does not put any submission to any of the witnesses which he knows to be false, does not mislead the court, and is not party to the formulation of any affirmative defence on behalf of the client. The practitioner may however attack the credibility of the witnesses, the admissibility and relevancy of the evidence, and the jurisdiction of the court and the form of the charge. The possibility of changing the plea to one of guilty must be kept in mind.

Related: [[Duty to the Court and Opposing Counsel]], [[Fees, Trust Deposits and Billing Practice]], [[Defending a Client Who Admits Guilt]]

## Q4 — Sole practitioner: trust theft and a prescribed claim (5)

> [!question] Question
> As a very busy single practitioner it has been brought to your notice by your firm's auditors that your bookkeeper has stolen a sum of R50 000,00 from the firm's trust account. At the same time your candidate attorney brings to your attention the fact that a large personal injury claim being handled by you has become prescribed. The client wishes to bring claims against you arising from the loss or prejudice suffered.
> Discuss in detail, in the above two instances, with whom any possible claims could be lodged and the procedural requirements for a valid claim or claims to be submitted.

> [!success]- Answer
> The first part deals with the indemnification of a member of the public by the Attorneys Fidelity Fund in respect of trust funds or other trust property entrusted to the practitioner in the course of his practice and stolen by the practitioner, his/her employee or candidate attorney. Here the bookkeeper is an employee of the practitioner, so a claim can be sustained against the Attorneys Fidelity Fund once the practitioner has been excussed.
>
> In the second instance a claim is lodged against the practitioner by the client whose claim has become prescribed, and the Professional Indemnity Insurance Fund provides indemnification to practitioners against a successful claim by a client arising from the practitioner's negligence. However, the practitioner is not permitted to admit liability to the client, but must refer the client to another practitioner.
>
> Candidates must also indicate understanding that not all trust monies enjoy the protection of the Attorneys Fidelity Fund, with reference to s47(1)(g) of the Act.

Related: [[Trust Account Theft and the Attorneys Fidelity Fund]], [[Prescribed Claims and the Attorneys Insurance Indemnity Fund]]
