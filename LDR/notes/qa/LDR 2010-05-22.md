## Q1 — Employee status and referring the dispute (8)

> [!question] Question
> Mario Smit consults you on his unfair dismissal — "simply kicked out" by his employer on 20 January 2010. He worked for The Heads Hairstylists on a commission basis only, earning on average R11 000/month over the last four months. No tax or UIF was ever deducted. The business is registered with a Hair Industry Bargaining Council. He worked 3 days/week, 08:00–14:00. He also did some perms/cuts at home for about R500/month, with the employer's knowledge and approval. He had certain managerial functions (scheduling other stylists), had no office at the business, no stationery/business cards issued, and used his own tools and equipment.
> 1.1 Advise Mario whether he is an employee in light of the facts and the law (5). 1.2 Assuming he was an employee, to which forum/court should he refer his unfair dismissal dispute (1)? 1.3 Which document(s) should be completed, served and filed when referring the dispute today (2)?

> [!success]- Answer
> 1.1 Could go either way — marks awarded on motivation with reference to the LRA, case law and the Code of Good Practice: Who is an Employee.
> **Arguments in favour of employee status (s200A presumption):** earnings (R11 000/month) fall below the Minister's threshold (then just over R149 000/year); worked more than 40 hours/month over the last 3 months (s200A(1)(d)); had managerial functions suggesting he formed part of the organisation (s200A(1)(c)); economically dependent on the business (s200A(1)(e)).
> **Arguments against:** paid on commission only, no fixed salary, no tax/UIF deducted; also did work for himself at home with the employer's knowledge (s200A(1)(g) — not exclusive to the business); provided his own tools and equipment (s200A(1)(f)); no office, stationery or business cards issued to him.
> A mark is also available for discussing the common-law tests: control test, dominant impression test, and/or organisation/integration test.
> 1.2 The Hair Industry Bargaining Council — the LRA requires referral to a registered bargaining council where one exists; only if there is no bargaining council does the dispute go to the CCMA.
> 1.3 The Referral of a Dispute (for Conciliation) form (bargaining council's own form, or CCMA Form LRA 7.11), plus an Application for Condonation supported by an affidavit.

## Q2 — Legal representation: CCMA arbitration vs internal hearing (6)

> [!question] Question
> Your client, Bheki Nzo, went through CCMA conciliation without resolution, requested arbitration, and has received a Notice of Set Down for arbitration in 2 weeks. He was dismissed for poor work performance and insists you represent him at the CCMA.
> 2.1 Is your client entitled to legal representation at the CCMA? Explain, and advise him what to do (3). 2.2 How does this differ from the right to legal representation at a normal internal disciplinary hearing? What would you advise (3)?

> [!success]- Answer
> 2.1 No, the client is not automatically entitled to legal representation. Rule 25 of the Rules for the Conduct of Proceedings before the CCMA allows a legal practitioner to appear at the CCMA on behalf of a party at arbitration, **unless** the dispute relates to the fairness of a dismissal and the reason for dismissal relates to the employee's conduct or capacity. Dismissal for poor work performance falls within "capacity". The exceptions in Rule 25(c)(1): unless the commissioner and all parties agree, or the employee files an application to be represented by a legal practitioner addressing the factors in Rule 25(c)(2).
> 2.2 In law, there is no right to be represented by a legal practitioner at an internal hearing — Item 4 of Schedule 8 refers only to a fellow employee or trade union representative. However, the employment contract, employer policies, or precedent on how this was previously handled may create such a right, in which case it can be enforced. One can always file an application to be represented and assisted by a legal practitioner — the appointed chairperson decides at the start of the hearing.

## Q3 — Onus of proof in unfair dismissal (3)

> [!question] Question
> By far, the majority of disputes referred to the CCMA are about unfair dismissals. Who bears the onus and what must be proved by the various parties?

> [!success]- Answer
> The employee must prove the existence of dismissal. The employer must then prove that the dismissal was fair (sections 192 and 188) — that is, that there was a fair reason and a fair procedure.

## Q4 — Non-appearance at arbitration, rescission (8)

> [!question] Question
> Mark Pienaar, a sole-proprietor plumber operating from his home in Bellville, uses a postal address for correspondence — no physical post box exists at his home. He dismissed an employee 6 months ago after a disciplinary hearing for theft of over R200 000. Yesterday the employee handed him an arbitration award ordering reinstatement with full benefits, 6 months' back pay, and R10 000 general damages. Your client never received any document or notice from the CCMA or the employee.
> 4.1 Advise your client on possible steps in terms of the LRA and CCMA Rules; what document(s) should be drafted and delivered, and within what period (2)? 4.2 You discover all documents were sent by registered mail to his home address at 6 Church Street. Draft the clauses dealing with reasons for non-appearance and prospects of success (6).

> [!success]- Answer
> 4.1 Apply for Rescission of the Arbitration Award in terms of s144 of the LRA and Rules 31 and 32 of the CCMA Rules. Document: Application/Notice of Motion and supporting affidavit(s).
> 4.2 **Reasons for non-appearance:** business run from home; uses postal address only; no post box outside; received no document or notice subsequent to the hearing from either the employee or the CCMA; would have opposed and attended the CCMA had he been notified; phoned the CCMA and established that registered mail was sent to his street address, but he never received anything because he has no post box there.
> **Prospects of success:** had a proper hearing — fair procedure followed with an outside/objective chairperson; serious offence of theft of R200 000 — substantively fair reason to dismiss; proved on a balance of probabilities; the chairperson recommended dismissal and the employer decided to dismiss; note the CCMA may not award general damages.
