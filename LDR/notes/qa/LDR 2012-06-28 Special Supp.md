## Q1 — Discrimination in recruitment: trade union membership (3)

> [!question] Question
> Your client applied for work at ACE Clothing but was unsuccessful. She believes she was discriminated against because of her membership of Unity, a registered trade union. The interview went well until a panellist asked whether she was a member of a trade union or planned to join one — she answered passionately, defending trade unions, and an argument ensued. She was not appointed and is not an "employee" as defined in s213 of the LRA, but seeks advice on her rights under the Act.
> 1.1 Advise her on referring a dispute to the appropriate forum, and explain the process from referral to the final order or award.

> [!success]- Answer
> Section 5(2)(c)(i) of the LRA: no person may prejudice a person seeking employment because of trade union membership. Section 9 of the LRA: refer the dispute to a council; if no council, then to the CCMA for conciliation. If the dispute remains unresolved, it may be referred to the Labour Court for adjudication. (Refer to Flow Diagram 1 of Schedule 4 of the LRA.)

## Q2 — Organisational rights and recognition (3)

> [!question] Question
> Your client, an employer, receives a written request/notice from a registered trade union seeking recognition as a representative union at the workplace, wanting to meet and exercise certain rights under the LRA. Employees have also approached the client about their rights to organise and bargain collectively. The union and members argue it is "sufficiently representative," though it does not represent a majority. The client refuses to attend to the requests and instructs you to keep the union off the premises. You fulfil your mandate, but the union and employees declare a dispute.
> 2.1 Describe to your client the dispute resolution process to be followed by the trade union and employees under the Act.

> [!success]- Answer
> Section 22 of the LRA: refer the dispute to the CCMA for conciliation; if unresolved, may request arbitration at the CCMA. [Alternatively, referring to section 21: the union may issue notice seeking to exercise certain rights; the employer must meet to negotiate a collective agreement; if no collective agreement is concluded, either party may refer the dispute to the CCMA for conciliation; if still unresolved, either party may request arbitration at the CCMA — this alternative answer also earns full marks.] (Refer to Flow Diagram 2 of Schedule 4 of the LRA.)

## Q3 — Poor work performance dismissal during probation, notice period (7)

> [!question] Question
> Robert was appointed by VP & Partners subject to a 3-month probationary period from 1 May 2012. He does not perform to the required standard of his job description, despite training and counselling, and has already been addressed informally by management. VP & Partners is your client. The employment contract is silent on the notice period.
> 3.1 VP & Partners wants to terminate Robert's employment. With reference to the LRA and the law of contract, advise your client whether the employer may terminate within the probation period, the basic requirements for a fair dismissal, and what notice should be given.

> [!success]- Answer
> Yes, the employer may terminate, provided there is a fair reason and a fair procedure under the LRA, specifically Item 8 of Schedule 8. If the employer determines the employee's performance is below standard, it should advise the employee of the respects in which performance fails to meet the required standard, or in which the employee is incompetent. The employer may only decide to dismiss after inviting the employee to make representations and considering those representations — the employee is entitled to state a case, and is entitled to be assisted by a union representative or fellow employee (who may also make representations on the employee's behalf). If the employer decides to dismiss, it must advise the employee of the right to refer a dispute to a council or the CCMA. A decision-maker ought to accept reasons that may be less compelling during probation than would be required for dismissals after completion of probation. The employer must give at least one week's written notice.

Related: [[Incapacity Dismissal for Poor Work Performance and Probation]]

## Q4 — Poor work performance dismissal (reproduced source question) (6)

> [!question] Question
> Mike is employed by X Computer Company and is still within his probation period. Despite proper training, he simply cannot perform as agreed contractually (per his job description). X Computer Company does not know what to do with him, as the situation at the workplace is unbearable.
> 4.1 Is X Computer Company entitled to terminate Mike's employment in these circumstances — could this be a fair reason? Explain briefly (1). 4.2 Assume X Computer Company is entitled to terminate — describe the procedure the company must follow (5).

> [!success]- Answer
> 4.1 Yes, incapacity due to poor work performance can be a valid and substantive reason to dismiss — whether within or outside the probation period.
> 4.2 The Code of Good Practice: Dismissal (Schedule 8, Items 4, 8 and 9) — after probation an employee should not be dismissed for unsatisfactory performance unless the employer has given appropriate evaluation, instruction, training, guidance or counselling, and after a reasonable period for improvement the employee continues to perform unsatisfactorily. The procedure should include an investigation into the reasons for unsatisfactory performance and consideration of ways short of dismissal to remedy the matter. The employee has the right to be heard and to be assisted by a trade union representative or fellow employee. The employee must be informed that performance doesn't meet the standard, told the consequences of non-improvement, and given an opportunity to improve before dismissal. The employer must supply proper support, guidance, supervision and training. A hearing is required before dismissal for poor work performance. Item 9 applies more directly post-probation; Item 8 allows a less formal and strict process during probation.

Related: [[Incapacity Dismissal for Poor Work Performance and Probation]]

## Q5 — Legal representation: internal hearing and CCMA (6)

> [!question] Question
> Your client receives a notice to appear at a disciplinary hearing, charged with misconduct. His "opponent," the initiator, is the employer's in-house legal adviser, a labour attorney of 20 years' standing. Your client wants you to represent him at the hearing. The employer has contracted a labour law consultant to chair.
> 5.1 Advise your client whether he is entitled to legal representation in these circumstances, with reference to legislation, case law, policies, precedents and/or his employment contract. Advise on steps to try to secure your representation (3).
> Your client is ultimately dismissed at the hearing and refers an unfair dismissal dispute to the CCMA. You inform him he is not entitled to legal representation at the CCMA; he does not accept this and instructs you to take steps to secure representation.
> 5.2 Briefly refer to the relevant legislation, rules, case law and/or policies, and advise your client what could be done (3).

> [!success]- Answer
> 5.1 In law, one does not have a right to be represented by a legal practitioner at the workplace — Item 4 of Schedule 8 refers: may be represented by a fellow employee or trade union representative only. However, the employment contract, employer policies, or precedent as to how this was dealt with previously by this specific employer may allow for it, in which case one can enforce it as a right. One can always bring an application to be represented and assisted by a legal practitioner — the appointed chairperson (not the employer, who is a party) decides.
> 5.2 The client is not entitled to legal representation in terms of the law, as he was dismissed for misconduct. Rule 25 of the Rules for the Conduct of Proceedings before the CCMA allows a legal practitioner to appear at the CCMA on behalf of a party at arbitration, unless the dispute relates to the fairness of a dismissal and the reason for dismissal relates to the employee's conduct or capacity. The exceptions are listed in Rule 25(c)(1): unless the commissioner and all parties agree, or the employee files an application to be represented by a legal practitioner addressing the factors in Rule 25(c)(2). [Or: simply prepare, counsel and train the client well to present his own case.]
