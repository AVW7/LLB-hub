## Q1 — Sale of a bottle store: security, conditions, restraint of trade, s34 notice (25)

> [!question] Question
> Mr Adam Smith sells his bottle store (run from leased premises) to John Brown "lock, stock and barrel" for R500 000. You act for the seller.
> 1.1 Advise on security for payment if paid via a R100 000 deposit and R10 000 monthly instalments. (5)
> 1.2 What suspensive conditions should be imposed? (5)
> 1.3 Draft an acceleration clause protecting the seller. (3)
> 1.4 Draft a two-year restraint-of-trade clause confining the seller from a similar business within Gauteng. (7)
> 1.5 Draft the s34 Insolvency Act sale notice for the newspaper. (5)

> [!success]- Answer
> 1.1 A notarial bond over the equipment sold in favour of the seller; cession of book debts; a third-party surety for the purchaser; a life policy on the purchaser (or a policy ceded to the seller) for the balance; and reservation of ownership plus an acceleration clause in the agreement.
> 1.2 Perfecting the lease agreement with the landlord; obtaining transfer of the liquor and trading licences.
> 1.3 On any missed payment, the full outstanding balance immediately becomes due, owing and payable.
> 1.4 The seller will not, for two years from the effective date, operate or hold any direct or indirect interest in a bottle store business in Gauteng, agreeing this restriction is fair and reasonable given the nature, duration and area of the restraint.
> 1.5 A notice under s34(1) of the Insolvency Act 24 of 1936 that Adam Smith intends to sell his business trading as [name], carried on at [address], to John Brown, after 30 days from the last publication.

## Q2 — Standard contract clauses (any three of four) (15)

> [!question] Question
> Draft any three of: 2.1 whole agreement (5); 2.2 variation (5); 2.3 jurisdiction of magistrates' courts (5); 2.4 severability of provisions (5).

> [!success]- Answer
> 2.1 Whole agreement: the document contains the entire agreement; neither party has rights arising from an undertaking, warranty or representation not included in it. (Note: sometimes unlawful/prohibited under consumer legislation — NCA s90, CPA s50(1)(g).)
> 2.2 Variation: the contract cannot be varied, added to or cancelled except by a further written agreement between the parties or their duly appointed agents. (Note: consumer legislation, e.g. NCA ss116–120, CPA s46, deals with variation separately.)
> 2.3 Jurisdiction: each party consents, under s45 of the Magistrates' Courts Act 1944, to the district magistrate's court's jurisdiction over proceedings under the agreement. (Note: from 15 October 2010 Regional Courts also have civil jurisdiction up to R300 000 in some instances — hence specifying "district"; unnecessary for credit agreements, where the Magistrate's Court has unlimited jurisdiction.)
> 2.4 Severability: each clause is separate and severable from the rest (unless severance would render the agreement unlawful or would be unreasonable given the price/clause/agreement as a whole); an unenforceable clause does not affect the rest of the agreement on the same proviso.

## Q3 — Consumer Protection Act remedies for a defective second-hand vehicle (15)

> [!question] Question
> Mr A bought a second-hand vehicle from a national dealer; four months later the gearbox broke. The defect existed at the date of purchase, and the car was not in good working order when bought.
> 3.1 With reference to consumer-protection legislation, advise on his rights and remedies. (12)
> 3.2 Would your advice differ if the agreement contained a voetstoots clause? (3)

> [!success]- Answer
> 3.1 Under ss55–56 of the CPA, every transaction implies a warranty from the producer/importer, distributor and retailer that the goods are reasonably suitable for their intended purpose, of good quality and free of defects, reliable and durable for a reasonable time, and compliant with the Standards Act. If goods fail this within 6 months of delivery, the consumer may return them without penalty, at the supplier's risk and expense, and the supplier must repair, replace, or refund. If a repair's defect recurs within 3 months, the supplier must replace or refund.
> 3.2 No — the supplier cannot contractually exclude the CPA's implied warranty.

Related: [[Consumer Protection Act]]

## Q4 — Suing on a prescribed claim; administering an oath with an interest (10)

> [!question] Question
> 4.1 A client insists you issue summons on a claim that has clearly prescribed. How do you explain the position to the client? (6)
> 4.2 Your partner has drawn an affidavit for an urgent High Court application and no Commissioner of Oaths is available. May you administer the oath? Would a conveyancing matter differ, and why? (4)

> [!success]- Answer
> 4.1 It's not wrong to issue summons on a claim you believe may fail on prescription; the client must be fully informed of the costs risk if unsuccessful, and this advice should be confirmed in writing. If prescription is pleaded as a special plea, the claim can be withdrawn before excessive costs accrue; if the Defendant doesn't raise it, the court may not raise it mero motu.
> 4.2 You may not administer the oath in a matter your firm is involved in, as you're then regarded as having an interest. Conveyancing matters are specifically excluded by the Act and Regulations from those where an interest is deemed to exist, so you may administer the oath there.

Related: [[Prescription of Claims]], [[Professional Ethics and Conduct]]

## Q5 — Types of companies and partnership essentialia (10)

> [!question] Question
> 5.1 What types of companies can be incorporated under the Companies Act 71 of 2008? (5)
> 5.2 What are the essentialia parties must agree on to form a valid partnership? (5)

> [!success]- Answer
> 5.1 Profit companies — state-owned, private, personal liability, or public — and non-profit companies (incorporated for a public benefit or similar object, akin to the old s21 company).
> 5.2 Each partner must contribute; all must share in profits and losses; the purpose must be to conduct business; the purpose must be to generate profit.

## Q6 — Sequestration of the husband: effect on a wife married out of community (5)

> [!question] Question
> A lady married out of community of property consults you; her husband, who holds most of her assets, is sequestrated. Advise on the effects under the Insolvency Act and possible remedies.

> [!success]- Answer
> Those assets vest first in the Master, then in the trustee. The spouse may obtain release of her assets by proving her ownership, usually by affidavit, and if necessary by a High Court application.

## Q8 — Letter to a partner after negligently allowing a claim to prescribe (10)

> [!question] Question
> You negligently allow a R200 000 damages claim (not personal injury) to prescribe. Your partner, overseas, asks you to explain what you did on discovering it and what the client's and the firm's rights/exposure are. Draft the letter.

> [!success]- Answer
> On discovering the prescription, you called the client in and informed him of the position, without admitting liability, and advised him to consult another attorney to assert his rights (the firm cannot continue to act, e.g. by hoping the defendant won't plead prescription). The client's new attorney will likely send a letter of demand, which is passed to the Attorneys Indemnity Insurance Fund; cover exceeds R200 000, so worst-case out-of-pocket exposure is the R20 000 excess. The AIIF typically instructs its own attorneys to defend or settle in consultation with the firm.

Related: [[Prescription of Claims]]

## Q9 — Recusal where the magistrate is the complainant's mother (5)

> [!question] Question
> Before a criminal trial where you represent the accused, you discover the magistrate is the complainant's mother. How do you apply the applicable ethical rules, and what do you do if unsuccessful?

> [!success]- Answer
> The magistrate must recuse herself. First raise it with her in chambers; if she declines, apply for recusal in open court; if she still refuses, proceed with the trial (no review/appeal at that stage). If the client is acquitted, no harm is done; if convicted, there are grounds for review.
