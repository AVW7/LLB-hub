# Prescription of Claims

Appears in both Part 3 (Attorney's Practice) papers in this sample, always from the **attorney's own risk-management perspective** — what to do when a claim has prescribed, or is at risk of it, rather than the substantive prescription periods themselves.

## Advising a client to sue on a claim that has clearly prescribed

It is **not wrong** to issue summons on a claim you believe will fail on prescription — but the client must be fully and clearly informed of the costs risk if the claim fails, and this advice should be **confirmed in writing** (clients often "forget" oral advice). If prescription is raised as a special plea, the claim can be withdrawn before excessive costs accrue; if the defendant doesn't raise it, the court will not raise it *mero motu*.

## When YOUR firm negligently lets a claim prescribe

**Protecting the client:**
- Advise the client **in writing** that the claim has prescribed.
- Do **not admit negligence** — this can vitiate cover under the firm's professional indemnity policy and the AIIF master policy.
- Advise the client to **consult another attorney** regarding both the original claim and the prescription issue — the firm cannot continue acting once its own conduct is in issue.

**Protecting yourself/the firm:**
- **Immediately** notify the firm's professional indemnity insurer and the **Attorneys Indemnity Insurance Fund (AIIF)** of the potential claim.
- Hand over any formal demand or summons received to the insurer(s).
- Expect the AIIF to instruct its own attorneys, who will defend or settle in consultation with the firm; exposure is typically limited to the policy excess.
