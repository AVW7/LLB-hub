# CLP — 21 July 2012

Source question paper: [[CLP21Jul2012Q.doc]]
Source marking memo: [[CLP21Jul2012A.doc]]

Facts: Mr Mohamed, a Tanzanian national, is wanted in New York on capital charges arising from the 1998 US embassy bombing in Dar es Salaam. The FBI traced him to Cape Town, where he was living under a false identity; South African immigration authorities arrested and interrogated him as an illegal immigrant and are due to hand him over to the FBI for removal to face a possible death sentence. This fact pattern closely tracks the real *Mohamed and Another v President of the RSA* Constitutional Court case.

## Q1 — Foreigner status and locus standi (3)

> [!question] Question
> Does the fact that Mr Mohamed is a foreigner preclude his protection under the Bill of Rights? Will he have locus standi before South African courts to enforce any human right?

> [!success]- Answer
> No. Foreigners can claim Bill of Rights protection — s7(1) enshrines the rights of "all people" in the country (including foreigners), entitled to all rights except those expressly reserved for citizens (e.g. s19(3), the right to vote). Anyone listed in s38 has the right to approach a competent court alleging infringement; Mohamed would have standing under s38(a), acting in his own interest.

Related: [[Application of the Bill of Rights to Foreigners]], [[Locus Standi]]

## Q2 — Rights infringed if handed over and sentenced to death (3)

> [!question] Question
> Assuming Mr Mohamed is handed over to the FBI and sentenced to death in the US, which of his constitutional rights are infringed by the South African government?

> [!success]- Answer
> His rights to **life**, **dignity**, and **not to be subjected to cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment**. (The memo notes candidates could also have raised whether he was advised of his right to legal counsel by SAPS/the FBI.)

Related: [[Limitation of Rights]]

## Q3 — Can government justify the infringement? (5)

> [!question] Question
> Counsel for the government argues the alleged infringement can be justified. Do you agree? Why?

> [!success]- Answer
> No. s36 governs limitation, but here the government would additionally be knowingly participating in facilitating cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment (a death sentence), which the Constitution forbids. The cornerstone is *S v Makwanyane* — capital punishment was found inconsistent with the interim Constitution's values, and no exception permitting it was later written into the 1996 Constitution; the values relied on in *Makwanyane* (dignity as both a right and a founding value) are carried through. The right to life has no textual exceptions, though (like all rights) it is subject to s36 — but the same s36 factors that made capital punishment unjustifiable in *Makwanyane* apply here. s12(1)(d)-(e) also protects freedom and security of the person, including not being tortured or treated/punished in a cruel, inhuman or degrading way. Handing Mohamed to a foreign government to face a possible death sentence, without first obtaining an assurance it will not be imposed/carried out, is inconsistent with the government's duty to protect life and its constitutional commitment against facilitating such punishment.

Related: [[Limitation of Rights]]

## Q4 — Direct access to the Constitutional Court (5)

> [!question] Question
> Mr Mohamed is due to be extradited in a week's time and would like to approach the Constitutional Court directly. What are the requirements under the Rules of the Constitutional Court, and will his application succeed?

> [!success]- Answer
> Given the urgency, an **urgent application for direct access** is needed — **Rule 12** (urgent applications): the Chief Justice may dispense with the ordinary forms/service and give bespoke directions, on notice of motion accompanied by an affidavit explicitly setting out the circumstances justifying departure from the ordinary procedure. Direct access itself is governed by s167(6) read with **Rule 18** — subject to the CC's leave, granted when in the interests of justice; ordinarily parties should first exhaust the usual court route (particularly in common-law matters), but urgency here supports direct access.

Related: [[Direct Access to the Constitutional Court]]

## Q5 — Amnesty International joining the proceedings (5)

> [!question] Question
> Amnesty International would like to make submissions on the constitutionality of Mohamed's deportation/extradition. How can it join the proceedings, and what is its role?

> [!success]- Answer
> As amicus curiae — not a party, no direct interest in the outcome, joins as "friend of the court," neither wins nor loses, generally no costs. Process: **Rule 10** of the Constitutional Court Rules (full subrules on consent-based or Chief-Justice-application-based admission, contents of the application, right to lodge written argument, limitation to the existing record, generally no oral argument).

Related: [[Amicus Curiae]]

## Q6 — Which remedy? (2)

> [!question] Question
> Which remedy(ies) would you apply for? Motivate your answer in detail.

> [!success]- Answer
> An interim interdict preventing Mohamed's handover pending the CC's decision; a declaratory that handover may only occur once the US gives an assurance he will not be put to death; and a mandamus forcing the government to obtain that assurance. Alternatively: a declaration that the arrest, detention, interrogation and handover were unlawful and unconstitutional, and that the respondents breached his rights by handing him over without first securing that assurance; and an order directing the SA government to submit a written request through diplomatic channels that the death penalty not be sought, imposed or carried out.

Related: [[Constitutional Remedies and Draft Orders]]

## Q7 — Draft the order (2)

> [!question] Question
> Assume Mr Mohamed was indeed handed over and removed to the US. Draft the order you require the Court to make (merits only, no need to deal with urgency).

> [!success]- Answer
> 1. It is declared that the handing over of Mohamed at Cape Town on or about [date] by agents of the South African government to agents of the United States, for removal to stand trial in the Federal Court for the Southern District of New York on charges carrying a possible death sentence, was unlawful in that it infringed his rights under sections 10, 11 and 12(1)(d) of the Constitution (dignity, life, and not to be treated/punished in a cruel, inhuman or degrading way), in that no prior undertaking was obtained from the US government that the death sentence would not be imposed or, if imposed, would not be executed.
> 2. There is no order as to costs.
> 3. The Director of this Court is authorised and directed to cause the full text of this judgment to be drawn to the attention of, and delivered to, the Director (or equivalent administrative head) of the Federal Court for the Southern District of New York, as a matter of urgency.
