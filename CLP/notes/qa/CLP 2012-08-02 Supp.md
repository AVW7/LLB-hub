# CLP — 2 August 2012 (Supp)

Source question paper: [[CLP 2012.2 SUPP ENG - only for appeal students.doc]]
Source marking memo: [[CLP2Aug2012A.doc]]

Facts: Charles Brown, having completed his academic requirements and attended the School for Legal Practice, wants to register his Articles of Clerkship with the Cape Law Society under s2(1A)(a) of the Attorneys Act 53 of 1979. As required by s5(2) of the Act, he discloses two previous convictions for possession of cannabis (dagga), and states he intends to continue using it — inspired by his Rastafarian religion. The Law Society declines to register his Articles.

## Q1 — Locus standi (4)

> [!question] Question
> Does Brown have locus standi and, if so, on what grounds, to bring legal action to compel the Law Society to register his Articles?

> [!success]- Answer
> Yes. Anyone listed in s38 has the right to approach a competent court. Brown falls within s38(a) — he has a personal interest in the matter. It could also be argued he acts on behalf of other Rastafarians under s38(c), provided he obtains their leaders' consent. *Ferreira v Levin NO* is cited as authority that the narrow common-law restrictions on standing don't apply to Bill of Rights litigation — Chapter 2 standing is broader, to protect a wider spectrum of people and rights.

Related: [[Locus Standi]]

## Q2 — Right of action / heads of argument (5)

> [!question] Question
> Does Brown have a right of action and, if so, what submissions of law would you include in your Heads of Argument?

> [!success]- Answer
> The Law Society's decision conflicts with s15(1) and s31(1) (freedom of religion, and the right to enjoy/practise culture and religion as a Rastafarian); s10 (the decision infringes his dignity); s22 (it infringes his right to pursue the profession of his choice); and s9 (he is subjected to unfair discrimination). Brown could also challenge the constitutionality of the legislation prohibiting dagga use even in a religious context.

Related: [[Limitation of Rights]]

## Q3 — Law Society's response and the "fit and proper" argument (5)

> [!question] Question
> Acting for the Cape Law Society: which of Brown's fundamental rights are possibly infringed, and what arguments would you raise in response? Briefly explain with reference to the applicable Bill of Rights section(s).

> [!success]- Answer
> The Law Society could argue that a person with two previous drug convictions, who declares an intention to continue breaking the law, is not a **fit and proper person** for admission — and that as long as the prohibition on dagga remains on the statute books, Brown will keep breaking the law, bringing the attorneys' profession into disrepute. Insisting on continuing to break the law is itself evidence of not being fit and proper. (The memo notes that arguing the State's case for why dagga should not be legalised is not relevant to this question.)

Related: [[Fit and Proper Person for Admission]]

## Q4 — Would the Magistrate's Court help? (5)

> [!question] Question
> Explain, with reference to relevant sections of the Constitution and applicable legislation, whether it would be advisable for Brown to approach a Magistrate's Court for relief.

> [!success]- Answer
> No. s170 of the Constitution: a court of a status lower than the High Court may not enquire into or rule on the constitutionality of any legislation or conduct of the President — this prohibits the Magistrate's Court from enquiring into or ruling on legislative constitutionality. s173: the Constitutional Court, SCA and High Courts have the inherent power to protect/regulate their own process and develop the common law. s110 of the Magistrates' Courts Act: a Magistrate's Court is not competent to pronounce on or deal with the validity of any law or conduct of the President. The competent court is the **High Court**, acting as a court of first instance. (See also the *Masiya* judgment, the male-rape case, flagged by the memo as a relevant reference point.)

Related: [[Jurisdiction in Constitutional Matters]]

## Q5 — Appeal procedure to the Constitutional Court (6)

> [!question] Question
> Assume the Law Society's decision was found constitutionally valid, the matter was appealed to the SCA, and the SCA confirmed the constitutional validity. Briefly explain the procedure to take the matter on appeal to the Constitutional Court.

> [!success]- Answer
> Appeals from the SCA to the Constitutional Court on a decision on a constitutional matter (other than an order of constitutional invalidity under s172(2)(a)) follow **Rule 19**. An aggrieved litigant must, within 15 days of the order (and after notice to the other parties), lodge an application for leave to appeal with the Registrar, signed by the applicant/representative, containing: the decision and grounds disputed; a statement of the constitutional matter (and any connected issues); supplementary information/argument; and a statement on whether leave/special leave was sought elsewhere, and its outcome. The respondent may respond within 10 days indicating opposition and grounds; a respondent wishing to cross-appeal must lodge that application within 10 days. The Court decides whether to grant leave — either summarily, or with argument directed to both leave and the merits.

Related: [[Leave to Appeal to the Constitutional Court]]
