# CLP — 30 January 2010

Source question paper: [[CLP30Jan2010Q.doc]]
Source marking memo: [[CLP30Jan2010A.doc]]

Facts: your client suffered severe burns and was left with a stroke and hemiplegia after negligent treatment at two Gauteng state hospitals. He obtained a High Court judgment against the MEC for Health for R1 496 000, but s3 of the State Liability Act 20 of 1957 bars execution/attachment against the State — the only route to payment is the National/Provincial Revenue Fund. He wants to challenge s3's constitutionality. The memo's model answer is built around the real Constitutional Court judgment in *Nyathi v MEC for the Department of Health, Gauteng*.

## Q1 — Rights infringed by s3 of the State Liability Act (3)

> [!question] Question
> Which of your client's constitutional rights (if any) are infringed by section 3 of the Act?

> [!success]- Answer
> Your client can challenge the constitutional validity of s3 on the basis that it violates, inter alia, the rights to **equality** (s9) and **dignity** (s10), and **access to courts** (s34, s165(5), s195(1)(f)). Any 3 will do.

Related: [[Limitation of Rights]]

## Q2 — Direct access to the Constitutional Court (4)

> [!question] Question
> Your client would like to apply for direct access to the Constitutional Court in terms of Rule 18 of the Rules of the Constitutional Court. What are the prospects of success of such an application and why?

> [!success]- Answer
> s167(6) of the Constitution, read with Rule 18, governs direct access — it is subject to the leave of the CC, granted when it is in the interests of justice. The CC's approach is that parties should first follow the usual route through the courts, because it is not ordinarily in the interests of justice for a court to sit as both court of first and final instance. [Full Rule 18 procedure reproduced in the memo: notice of motion + affidavit; lodged with the Registrar and served on interested parties; must set out grounds for interest of justice, nature/grounds of relief, and whether oral evidence is needed; opposition within 10 days; disposed of per Chief Justice directions, summarily unless opposed.]

Related: [[Direct Access to the Constitutional Court]]

## Q3 — Arguments to have s3 declared invalid (8)

> [!question] Question
> Which arguments would you forward to have section 3 of the Act declared invalid? Motivate your answer in detail.

> [!success]- Answer
> The memo answer is the applicant's argument as it appears in the real *Nyathi* judgment: s3 is unconstitutional because it prevents attachment of State assets despite a court order and should be struck down; a contempt order against a nominal defendant is not an appropriate or effective remedy — execution is; the word "may" in s3 is discretionary, and even read as "must," an Act of Parliament would still be needed to enable a claim against the Funds; s213(2) of the Constitution precludes drawing on the Funds absent enabling legislation; State inefficiency makes enforcing contempt proceedings difficult since the responsible official often cannot be identified; s3 unjustifiably differentiates between a judgment creditor against the State and one against a private litigant (contrary to s9(1) equality), and undermines ss8, 34 and 165 (binding nature of court orders, access to courts, judicial authority).

## Q4 — Amicus curiae: the Centre for Constitutional Rights (5)

> [!question] Question
> The Centre for Constitutional Rights (the CFCR) would like to become amicus curiae. What is the role of the amicus curiae and what must the CFCR show in their application to become amicus curiae?

> [!success]- Answer
> An amicus curiae is not a party to the litigation (no direct interest in the outcome), but believes the court's decision may affect its interests — it joins as "a friend of the court," is neither winner nor loser, and is generally not entitled to costs. Process: Rule 10 of the Constitutional Court Rules. With written consent of all parties (lodged with the Registrar within 5 days), a person may be admitted on agreed terms; without consent, application is made to the Chief Justice. The application must briefly describe the amicus's interest, identify the position it will adopt, and set out the submissions it will advance, their relevance, and why they will be useful and different from the other parties'. The amicus may lodge written argument (no repetition of other parties' argument, new contentions only), is limited to the existing record, and (subject to rule 31) may not present oral argument.

## Q5 — Does the High Court's invalidity order have force? (5)

> [!question] Question
> The High Court declares part of s3 of the State Liability Act inconsistent with the Constitution and invalid, and orders costs on the attorney-client scale. Does this order have any immediate force? If you would like it to have any force, what should happen and how can you see to it?

> [!success]- Answer
> No — s172 of the Constitution provides that an order of constitutional invalidity of an Act of Parliament has no force unless confirmed by the Constitutional Court. The procedure for confirmation is set out in Rule 16(4) of the CC Rules: the Registrar of the court that made the order lodges a copy with the CC Registrar within 15 days; a party wishing to appeal against the order (s172(2)(d)) or apply for confirmation must do so within 15 days; if neither is lodged in time, the matter is disposed of per directions of the Chief Justice. [The memo attaches the actual *Nyathi v MEC for the Department of Health, Gauteng* CC judgment: the order of constitutional invalidity was confirmed, but the declaration of invalidity was suspended for 12 months to give Parliament time to pass legislation providing for effective enforcement of court orders.]
