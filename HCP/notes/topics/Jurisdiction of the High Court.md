# Jurisdiction of the High Court

The court's legal competence to hear a specific matter and grant an order in it. Tested constantly as a "spot the mistake" issue embedded in drafting scenarios.

## The three grounds

- **Ratione domicilii (personae)** — the defendant resides or is domiciled within the court's area.
- **Ratione rei gestae (causae)** — the cause of action arose within the court's area.
- **Ratione contractus** — the contract was concluded (or performable) within the court's area.

## Statutory basis (old Act s19 / current position)

- **Founding jurisdiction**: plaintiff must reside within the court's area, or the defendant resides/is present there.
- **Confirming jurisdiction**: the cause of action must arise in the area — except where the defendant is resident in South Africa (s28 of the old Act), in which case residence alone suffices without the cause of action having to arise there.
- A **domicilium citandi et executandi** address is *not* on its own enough to found jurisdiction — *Geyser v Nedbank* 2006 (4) SA 544.
- **s21 (old Act)** — a court may **attach property** (or an incorporeal, e.g. a debt owed to the defendant by a local third party) within its area to **found or confirm jurisdiction** over a *peregrinus* defendant. Procedure: ex parte application for an order calling on the local debtor to show cause why the debt should not be attached to found jurisdiction.
- Practical rule of thumb from the exam memos: attaching a debt owed by a local company to a foreign (peregrinus) defendant lets you sue that defendant here even though neither party nor the cause of action is otherwise local.

## Mag Court vs High Court jurisdiction consequences

- If the matter falls within Magistrates' Court jurisdiction but is brought in the High Court, the plaintiff may only recover **Magistrates' Court scale costs** (subject to the High Court's discretion) — *Oosthuizen v Road Accident Fund* 2011 (6) SA 3 (SCA).
- The High Court also has **inherent jurisdiction**.

## Common jurisdiction-error fact pattern (exam favourite)

Plaintiff resides in area A; defendant (a *peregrinus*) resides in area B; the collision/cause of action arose in area B — a summons issued from A's court is **fatally defective**: neither the plaintiff's residence (irrelevant to founding jurisdiction over the defendant) nor the cause of action (arose in B) supports A's jurisdiction. See [[HCP 2010-03-06]] Q1.

## Related — locus standi is a *separate* defect

Jurisdiction (does *this court* have power) is routinely tested together with, but is legally distinct from, **locus standi** (does *this plaintiff/defendant* have standing) — e.g. suing for damage to a vehicle you don't own, or suing for bodily injury the RAF should carry. See [[Particulars of Claim and Rule 18 Requirements]].
