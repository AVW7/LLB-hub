# Review Procedure and Appeal vs Review

## Appeal vs review — the core distinction (as tested)

- **Appeal**: normally a rehearing on the merits (subject to any court order), but limited to the evidence/information on which the original decision was given; the appeal court is **bound by the record**.
- **Review**: concerned with **grave irregularities** in the proceedings of a lower court/tribunal — not with the correctness of the decision on the merits, but with the **fairness/lawfulness of the process** by which it was reached.
- Time limits: appeal must generally be brought within a **particular period**; review may be brought within a **reasonable time**.
- Grounds: appeal grounds are effectively unlimited (any wrong conclusion on the facts or the law); review grounds are **limited** to defects in the proceedings themselves.

## Grounds of review — s22 of the Superior Courts Act (from lower courts)

1. Absence of jurisdiction.
2. Interest in the cause (of the presiding officer).
3. Bias.
4. Malice.
5. Corruption.
6. Gross irregularity in the proceedings.
7. Admission of inadmissible or incompetent evidence.
8. Rejection of admissible or competent evidence.

## Review procedure — Rule 53

1. Applicant obtains the **record** from the relevant registrar/clerk and makes copies for each party plus two for the court; the attorney certifies the record as a true copy.
2. Notice of motion and affidavit served on the presiding officer/tribunal and all parties, calling on them to **show cause** why the decision should not be reviewed and set aside, and requiring despatch of the record and reasons.
3. The applicant may, within **10 days** of receiving the record, **amend** the notice of motion.
4. After the presiding officer's explanatory affidavit is filed, the applicant sets the matter down like an ordinary opposed application, heard by a **single judge** on any motion day.
5. No formal time limit to institute review, but it must be brought within a **reasonable time**.

## Related note — internal review remedies

Where a statute provides an internal review/dispute-resolution mechanism (e.g. mediation under enabling legislation), a party is generally expected to have exhausted or properly engaged with it before resorting to urgent court relief — and separately, a party aggrieved by an administrative decision should generally proceed by a **review application under the Promotion of Administrative Justice Act 3 of 2000 (PAJA)**, not by treating the matter as an urgent mandamus, where PAJA review was the appropriate route from the outset.
