# Summary Judgment — Rule 32

The single most frequently drafted application in the module.

## When competent — Rule 32(1)

Only for four types of claim:
1. A claim on a liquid document.
2. A claim for a liquidated amount in money.
3. A claim for delivery of specified movable property.
4. A claim for ejectment.

Not competent for a damages claim (unliquidated) — a recurring trap, see [[HCP 2010-03-06]] Q1.

## Sequence

- Available once the defendant has delivered a Notice of Intention to Defend.
- Application made 15 days after NID delivered (or 20 days after summary judgment was previously refused, under Rule 32(8A) if renewed).

## The supporting affidavit — two required opinions

The deponent (who must be able to swear positively to the facts, i.e. usually the plaintiff or someone with personal knowledge) must state, in their opinion:
1. The defendant has no bona fide defence to the action; and
2. The notice of intention to defend has been delivered solely for the purpose of delay.

### Drafting checklist — Affidavit in support of summary judgment (verbatim pattern from the memos)

I, the undersigned, [name], do hereby make oath and say:
1. I am the plaintiff in this case. I have personal knowledge of the facts contained in the summons.
2. I confirm the cause of action and the amount claimed in the summons.
3. I verily believe the defendant has no bona fide defence to the action and has delivered notice of intention to defend solely for purposes of delay.

Note across markers: no additional evidence beyond the summons may be tendered in the affidavit.

## The Maharaj test (defendant resisting on the papers)

Per Corbett JA in Maharaj v Barclays National Ltd 1976 (1) SA 418 (A) at 426, the court does not try to resolve factual disputes or weigh probabilities; it enquires only into:
1. Whether the defendant has fully disclosed the nature and grounds of the defence and the material facts on which it is founded; and
2. Whether, on the facts so disclosed, the defendant appears to have, as to the whole or part of the claim, a defence that is both bona fide and good in law.

## Defendant's two avenues to resist

1. File an opposing affidavit setting out a bona fide defence; or
2. Furnish security for any order the court may make (e.g. the amount claimed plus costs). If the parties cannot agree the form/amount of security, the Registrar decides, and that decision is final and binding — there is no appeal from it.
